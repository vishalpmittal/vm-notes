# Career Insider Trading (the Legal Kind)

## Key Takeaways

- As an employee you hold **inside information** about your company, your industry, and yourself that outsiders (analysts, journalists, the job market) don't have yet. The edge is *acting on it before it becomes public*.
- Insider trading is illegal in the stock market because it works too well. The **career version is legal** — and most people never use it.
- People fail to act because they wait for one of three confirmations that all arrive too late: **the numbers**, **an official statement**, or the feeling that the move is finally **"justified."** By the time it's justified, everyone knows and the edge is gone.
- **Career trades are small and concrete** — spend learning hours on next-role skills, take a recruiter call while employed, get coffee with a winning team, aim a job search at the growing corner you personally know about.
- In 2026, **AI is the highest-value inside signal**: your on-the-ground view of what AI does and can't do in your role beats the company's official (delayed, softened) story.

## The Nokia Analogy

By 2007, Nokia engineers internally knew they couldn't compete with the iPhone — while the outside world still saw Nokia as the untouchable giant. The few who "traded" on that private information (retrained, moved to Apple/Google *while Nokia's name still opened doors*) won. Those who waited were dumped into a saturated job market all at once in 2013.

## Why Nobody Trades on It

We wait for three confirmations, and each comes late:

1. **The numbers arrive late.** Nokia had a *record year* in 2007; the cracks took 3–4 years to show in the financials.
2. **The official statement never comes.** Researchers of Nokia's collapse found middle managers feared passing bad news upward (executives shot the messenger), so "we're in serious trouble" became "we're on track" by the time it reached the top.
3. **"Now it's justified" comes last.** Once the move is obviously correct to everyone, it's no longer an edge. *The edge was when the move was still a bit early.*

## What "Using It" Looks Like

Three situations where people sit on an edge instead of trading it:

- **Things are bad but comfortable** — product stagnant or being eaten by AI, best people leaving, constant reshuffles. *Trade:* move toward faster-growing competitors, or build the skills your next role needs.
- **Move closer to the "good"** — get near a winning internal initiative *before* it becomes the competitive center and everyone wants in. *Trade:* volunteer, get coffee, attach yourself early.
- **Job searching** — most people apply to big brand names. *Trade:* aim your search at the growing corner where you personally know the opportunities are.

Sometimes the trade is realizing **you're in a good spot** (product improving, good people joining, bad projects getting killed) — in which case you can ignore scary industry headlines, because your information beats any journalist's.

## Diagnostics Only an Insider Can Run

If your read is fuzzy, run checks outsiders can't:

- **Customer energy** — do customers still genuinely love the product?
- **Pivot speed** — does a failing thing die in a quarter, or limp along for two years? Fast-admitting companies handle shifts like AI far better.
- **AI specifically** — does leadership *personally use* AI, or just sponsor it? (A CEO setting mobile strategy in 2008 who never held a smartphone is a warning sign.) Then map which parts of your own role AI already does vs. can't touch.
