# Evaluating Advice: What to Do When Smart People Disagree

Two smart, successful people will often give you the exact opposite advice — and both can be completely right. Steve Jobs told Stanford grads to follow your passion and keep looking until you find it. Mark Cuban says the opposite: don't follow your passion, follow your effort, because you end up loving whatever you get good at. They're both right — *about themselves*. Jobs only ever did well at things he was obsessed with; Cuban gets hooked on anything he starts winning at. The task isn't to pick a winner. It's to work out which rule fits *you*.

## Key Takeaways

- **Advice is a solution to someone else's problem.** Don't copy the rule — understand why it worked for that person (what problem were they solving, what was true about their situation), then ask the obvious question: is that true for me too?
- **The same advice reverses with context.** "Say yes to everything" is great when your career has gone quiet or you're early; "say no to almost everything" is right when you already have ten things you can't do justice. Neither is universal.
- **Start with the boring answer.** When many directions confuse you, ask what the simplest, most obvious answer is — then check whether you're actually doing it. Boring platitudes get ignored because the obvious doesn't sell, not because they're wrong.
- **Run a small experiment instead of taking someone's word.** Before a big decision, ask: "Can I just find out for myself?" Cheap real data beats confident opinion.
- **Discount charismatic certainty.** People routinely mistake confidence for competence. Train an immune response: strip away the conviction and inspect the actual argument and evidence underneath.
- **Use AI as a sparring partner, not an oracle.** It can argue any side convincingly, so use it to argue *against* your leaning and surface your assumptions — not to hand you an answer.

## Does This Apply to Me?

When you hear advice, or someone tells you what to do, don't just copy or ignore it. Reconstruct *why* it worked for them:

- What problem were they solving?
- What was true about their situation, temperament, or stage?
- Is that true for me too?

Knowing your own wiring makes this faster — how you naturally work, what gives you energy, how you decide, and which environments bring out your best. The better you understand yourself, the easier it is to tell when someone else's advice fits and when it doesn't. Don't assume that what works for someone else is even what you'll *enjoy*.

## Start With the Boring Answer

When you're stuck between many competing directions, ask: **what's the boring, obvious answer here?**

The classic example is weight loss. You can debate keto, fasting, meal timing, carbs, and exercise routines for months — but the boring answer is "eat less," which everyone already knows (that's what makes it boring). Tellingly, Ozempic — arguably the biggest weight-loss breakthrough in decades — mostly just makes you eat less. Billions in R&D landed on the platitude.

Boring advice gets ignored because an entire industry is built on advice, and the obvious doesn't sell. When stuck: *What's the simplest answer here? Am I actually doing it? If not, why not?*

## Can I Just Find Out?

Instead of taking advice on faith or dismissing it, run a small experiment and get your own answer.

- **Example:** Content creators say (unsurprisingly) that creating content is the best thing for your career. Rather than assume, test it — post consistently, grow a following, and watch what it actually does for you. The author's verdict: useful, but not worth the same ongoing energy — so he scaled back and stopped wondering about it.
- **Make it cheap and small first:** thinking of switching careers? Do a small paid gig at a reduced rate. Want to build a company? Use AI to prototype and try to market it before committing.

Before any big decision, ask: *what could I do that would give me actual data?*

## Discount Charismatic Certainty

The brain routinely mistakes confidence for competence. A 2013 study found people rated high-confidence statements as far more credible — even when the speaker was demonstrably wrong and had no evidence. When someone speaks with absolute conviction in a boardroom or on a podcast, your own read wobbles ("they sound so sure — maybe I'm backward").

Build an immune response to it: when someone speaks with zero nuance, mentally discount the certainty and look at the argument underneath. *What do they actually know? What evidence do they have?* (This is the flip side of [spotting BS](../leadership/spotting-bs.md) — the same skill of separating fluency from truth.)

## Use AI to Argue the Other Side

AI can make almost any position sound convincing — a great case for quitting *and* a great case for staying. So it's more useful as a sparring partner than an oracle. When leaning one way, ask it to:

- Make the strongest case for the *opposite*
- Point out what you might be assuming
- Tell you what information would change the answer

This keeps you grounded and lets you make up your own mind, rather than outsourcing the decision.
