# Managing Nerves

## Key Takeaways

- Nerves measure stakes, not skill — they show up when you care about the result, can't be sure of it, and are on unfamiliar ground; "how good you are" isn't on that list, which is why practice never fully cures them
- If you're nervous, you're by definition doing something new and uncertain that you care about — i.e. you're growing; a life with no nerves usually means a life spent only on the already-known
- Your nervousness is mostly invisible — the "illusion of transparency" means audiences barely notice what feels obvious to you; just knowing this measurably improves performance
- Don't try to get calm (too far a jump); relabel the identical physical state as *excitement* — saying "I am excited" out loud beats "I am calm"
- Nerves are a career gauge: what triggers them moves to the edge of what you've done, so bigger triggers over time = growth

## Why Practice Doesn't Cure Nerves

You don't get nervous ordering coffee — you know how it goes and don't care much. Nerves appear when three things are true at once: you **care about the result**, the **result is uncertain**, and you're on **new, unfamiliar ground**. Skill isn't on that list. Practice makes you better at the thing, but it can't make you care less or make the outcome certain. (Adele has thrown up before shows and once fled through a fire exit rather than go on stage — thousands of performances didn't fix it, because she still wants tonight's crowd and still can't be sure of them.)

Corollary: elderly people's regrets are overwhelmingly things they *didn't* do. Nerves sit right before the moments that matter, so treating them as a stop sign means stopping exactly where you'd later regret it.

## Actionable Insights

**Slow down — speed is the one thing people *can* see.** Nervous people talk fast because silence feels dangerous; it's the biggest tell. Talking at roughly half your natural pace feels ridiculous from the inside and sounds like total control from the outside. Pauses let points land.

**Relabel, don't calm down.** Calm (low adrenaline, slow heart) is the opposite physical state to nervous — you can't cross that gap in two minutes. Excitement is physically *identical* to nervousness; only the label differs. Alison Wood Brooks (Harvard) found people who said "I am excited" before speaking and math tests outperformed those who said "I am calm." Your body won't believe "calm"; it'll happily believe "excited." Whatever's left, welcome it as proof you're growing.

**Nail the opening.** Nerves peak *before* you start, then fade once you're going — so the opening is the only part they actually touch:
- In a meeting, say something in the first few minutes; the longer you stay silent, the harder speaking gets.
- Before a presentation, script your first minute word-for-word.
- Meeting new people, prepare a strong intro / elevator pitch so the first minute runs smoothly.

**Name it out loud.** In many rooms you can just say "wow, I didn't expect to be this nervous" — it disarms both you and the room.

**Use nerves as a self-check.** Are you nervous about bigger things than you were three years ago? If yes, you're growing. If the same things still scare you — or nothing does, because everything you do is something you've done before — that's a signal you've settled into the already-known.
