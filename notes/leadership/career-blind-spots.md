# Career Blind Spots

## Key Takeaways

- The information you need is usually available — what's missing is the willingness to look, because looking has a price: your expertise, comfort, or the story you tell about your own path
- **Success is a sedative.** The better you are at the old way, the stronger the urge to dismiss what threatens it — so treat your own dismissal as an alarm, not a verdict
- Your view of your industry is only as good as your inputs; if everything arrives secondhand through your company, you inherit its blind spots
- Judge a trend by its **speed, not its snapshot** — "how fast is it improving?" beats "is it good today?"
- Your blind spots are invisible only to you — the people around you can diagnose them in seconds, but you have to ask

## The Core Problem

WW1 generals were the best military minds of their generation — which was exactly the problem. They'd won real wars with cavalry, so when the machine gun arrived they kept drilling soldiers to charge across open ground and canceled the order for the tank. The truth was expensive, so they refused to look. Blind spots work the same way in a career: the tank is usually right in front of you.

## Four Blind Spots (and the Fix for Each)

**1. We dismiss whatever threatens us.** In 1975 a Kodak engineer built the first digital camera; management said "interesting, now put it away." Kodak didn't miss digital — they *invented* it and buried it, because taking it seriously meant admitting film would die.
→ *Fix:* When you catch yourself scoffing at something new in your field ("AI code is garbage," "no serious client would buy that way"), give it one honest hour — use the tool, read the report, talk to someone actually doing it. If it's nothing, you lost an hour. If it isn't, you found the exact spot you were choosing not to look.

**2. We only see what our inputs show us.** Michael Burry made $700M shorting the 2008 housing market not because he was smarter than Wall Street's quants, but because he read the actual mortgage documents while everyone else read ratings and headlines.
→ *Fix:* Every couple of months, read the job postings for your role at other companies — they're companies publicly declaring which skills they'll pay for next. Once a quarter, talk to someone who left your company or field; they have no reason to keep telling the official story.

**3. We judge what things are, not what they're becoming.** Kodak's buried camera was genuinely bad — 8 pounds, blurry black-and-white, 23 seconds to save one photo to a cassette. But "is it good?" was the wrong question. Film hadn't changed in decades; digital was doubling every two years.
→ *Fix:* Compare a thing to itself two years ago, not to your standards today. If it's improving fast, it will clear your bar soon enough — better early than surprised. People who try AI once, get a mediocre result, and call it overhyped have judged the snapshot instead of the speed.

**4. Everyone can see your blind spots except you.** You can diagnose a friend's career in seconds; they can do the same for yours — but nobody asks.
→ *Fix:* Ask close friends or people adjacent to your industry: *"What's something about my career/industry that's obvious to you, but I probably can't see?"* People usually have an answer ready. Borrow the newest eyes around you too — juniors and outsiders spot shifts precisely because they have nothing invested in the old way.

## Actionable Insights

- **Treat the urge to scoff as a diagnostic.** Strong emotion about something new means you've likely stopped analyzing it and started protecting yourself from it.
- **Give any dismissal one honest hour** before you're allowed to keep dismissing it.
- **Upgrade your inputs on a schedule:** job postings every ~2 months, an ex-colleague/ex-industry conversation every quarter.
- **Ask "how fast is it improving?"** not "is it good?" when sizing up a new tool, skill, or trend.
- **Solicit your blind spots directly** — ask friends and juniors the "what can't I see?" question.
- **Run the sell test:** would you take your current job today, knowing what you know? If the honest answer is no, that's information.
