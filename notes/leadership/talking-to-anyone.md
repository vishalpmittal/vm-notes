# Talking to (Almost) Anyone

## Key Takeaways

- With a best friend you can talk for hours; with a stranger you're checking your phone in four minutes. Same you — so the difference was never personality. It's two conditions: **having a live topic** and **not monitoring yourself**. Both are learnable
- **Curiosity and self-consciousness compete for the same attention** — you only get one. Spend it on the other person, not on grading yourself
- Every answer should hand the other person something to grab; a specific detail does their follow-up work for them
- People you "can't talk to" are usually people whose live topic you haven't found yet — most quit one question too early
- **Depth isn't found in a conversation, it's offered.** Someone has to go first, and people match the level of realness you set
- Being good with people is a **multiplier**, not a base — it multiplies whatever you're actually good at, so get genuinely good at something first

## Get Out of Your Own Head

Hard conversations are hard because you're watching yourself have them — editing every sentence, which is exactly what makes you flat. Two research findings make it easier to drop the self-monitoring:

- **Spotlight effect** (Cornell): students in an embarrassing t-shirt guessed half the room would remember it; fewer than a quarter did.
- **Liking gap:** after talking, strangers consistently underestimate how much the other person liked them.

Nobody is grading you as hard as you are, and the average conversation goes better than you think. Spend the attention on them instead.

## Give Them Something to Grab

A conversation survives only if each answer gives the other person something to respond to. "How was your weekend?" / "Good. You?" leaves them nothing. Answer with one specific detail: "Good — I tried a pottery class and made something genuinely hideous." Now they have three easy follow-ups. Apply this hardest to "What do you do?" — "I'm in marketing" sounds like everyone; "I run marketing for a company that sells hot sauce to firefighters" starts the conversation by itself.

## Drill Until You Hit a Live Topic

The quiet accountant is dull about accounting and electric about rock climbing. The tool is the follow-up question: when an answer has any energy, ask a second question about the *same* thing, then a third ("How did you end up doing that?"). Hopping between topics is what makes conversations feel like interviews. When their energy visibly jumps, you've struck the topic — stay on it.

## Someone Has to Go First

Small talk drags not from lack of chemistry but because both people wait for the other to say something real first, so both stay safe and the conversation stays beige. In a famous study, strangers who answered gradually more personal questions felt closer after 45 minutes than many long-time acquaintances. One honest admission is plenty: "Honestly, I almost skipped tonight — I barely know anyone here." The person who "can talk to anyone" is mostly just the person willing to go first.

## The Actually Hard Cases

- **The one-word answerer** → switch from questions to statements. Questions demand effort from someone giving none; an observation or small story ("I heard the offsite got rained out — they played poker in a parking garage for four hours") costs them nothing and shows them how to hold up their end.
- **The intimidating senior person** → everyone else either performs or interviews them about work. The memorable move is being *normal*: ask the human question others are too nervous to ask, react honestly, disagree lightly. You won't out-credential a VP — be the one relaxed conversation in their day.
- **The cold open** → the opener's only job is to exist. Nobody remembers first lines, only whether the conversation went anywhere. Comment on whatever you're both looking at ("How do you know the host?").

## One Thing Talking Can't Do

People skills multiply your impact (think core skill × people skills = impact) — they get your work noticed, remembered, and recommended. But a multiplier needs something to multiply: if you ever have to choose, get genuinely good at something first. The more common mistake runs the other way, though — talented people who assume the work speaks for itself, and lose to slightly less talented people everyone enjoys having in the room.

## Actionable Insights

- **Redirect attention outward** when you feel self-conscious — curiosity crowds out the internal monologue.
- **Never answer with a dead end** — attach one specific, grab-able detail to even "how was your weekend?"
- **Craft a specific answer to "what do you do?"** that starts a conversation on its own.
- **Follow the energy, not a checklist** — when a topic lights someone up, ask the next question about *that*.
- **Go first on realness** in the right settings; a small honest admission unlocks the same from others.
- **With intimidating people, be the normal one.** With one-word answerers, use statements not questions.
